Triggers vs Core Drivers: The Two-Layer System Behind Every Impulse Purchase

📅 6 min read 🏷 impulse spending · psychology · emotional spending core drivers

When we try to stop impulse spending, we usually start with the surface.

We unsubscribe from sale emails. We delete the shopping apps. We avoid the high street on payday. We remove the trigger, and for a while it works — until the urge finds a different route to the same destination. A new newsletter arrives. A friend's Instagram becomes the window display. Payday still feels like permission, even without the app.

The trigger wasn't the problem. It was just the match. What we never dealt with was the fire underneath it.

Every impulse purchase has two layers. Most advice about impulse spending addresses only the first. This article is about both — what they are, how they interact, and why understanding the combination is the thing that actually changes behaviour.

The Trigger

What a trigger actually is

A trigger is any event — external or internal — that activates the impulse to buy. It's the thing that sets the sequence in motion. Crucially, it's situational: it arises from your environment, your context, or the state you happen to be in. You don't choose it. It happens to you.

Triggers come in ten main forms:

📱 browsing / scrolling 😰 stress 😐 boredom 🌙 late night 💰 payday 📣 advertising 👥 social / FOMO 📧 email promotions 🏷 sale / discount 🔁 habit / autopilot 🎉 celebration 😞 low mood

Some are external — a notification, a sale, an ad that followed you across three websites. Some are internal — a feeling of restlessness, a specific time of day, the moment your salary lands. Both are triggers in the same sense: they create the conditions in which an impulse arises.

The important thing to understand about triggers is that they are not the problem. They are unavoidable features of ordinary life. You will encounter sale emails and payday and late evenings with your phone in your hand for the rest of your life. Removing triggers is a partial and temporary solution — one that addresses the surface without touching what the trigger was activating underneath.

the useful question Not "how do I avoid this trigger?" but "what does this trigger activate in me — and is that need actually being met by the purchase?"

What a core driver is

A core driver is the underlying emotional need that a purchase is attempting to satisfy. Where triggers are external and situational, core drivers are internal and personal. They come from within — from the particular emotional state you're in, the needs that aren't currently being met, the feelings you'd like to move toward or away from.

There are ten core drivers. None of them are character flaws. Every one of them is a legitimate human need. The issue isn't that you have them — it's that spending has become the automatic response to them.

🌬
Relief
Spending as a pressure valve. The urge arises when tension or stress has been building and the purchase offers a moment of release — a breath out after a held breath in. It doesn't resolve what caused the pressure. It briefly lets some out.
🎁
Reward
Buying as a treat you feel you've earned. Often follows effort, difficulty, or deprivation — "I've had a hard week" or "I've been so good lately." The purchase functions as self-administered recognition for something the outside world hasn't acknowledged.
🚪
Escape
Using shopping to exit a feeling or situation, even temporarily. The scroll becomes a way out of an uncomfortable evening, a difficult emotion, or a task you don't want to return to. You're not buying the thing — you're buying the departure from where you currently are.
🤗
Comfort
The soothing quality of browsing or owning something new. Comfort purchases often happen in moments of low mood, loneliness, or physical discomfort — the purchase offers a small, immediate warmth that temporarily addresses the cold. What's actually needed is usually something softer and harder to acquire.
✨
Validation
Buying to feel approved of, seen, or worthy. Validation purchases are often tied to social context — buying something to show others, to signal belonging, or to feel that you measure up. Sometimes it's approval from others; sometimes it's a form of permission granted to yourself.
⚡
Excitement
The dopamine loop of wanting and acquiring. The excitement driver is about the feeling of anticipation — the wanting — as much as the thing itself. It's why unboxing videos work, and why post-purchase disappointment is so common: the peak was the wanting, not the having.
🤝
Connection
Buying to feel closer to a community, a person, or a movement. Connection purchases often follow a sense of being left out or behind — seeing what others have, buying into a shared aesthetic or interest. The purchase is a bridge toward belonging.
🎛
Control
Spending as a way to exert agency when other things feel uncertain. When work, relationships, or circumstances feel out of your hands, making a purchase — making a decision, choosing something — can restore a brief sense of autonomy. It's one of the reasons impulse spending peaks during stressful or uncertain periods.
🪞
Identity
Purchasing as a way of becoming or reinforcing who you want to be. Identity purchases are often aspirational — buying the version of yourself you're working toward, or maintaining the version you've already built. The item is less important than what owning it says about who you are.
⏳
FOMO
Buying to avoid the discomfort of missing out. FOMO purchases are driven less by wanting the thing and more by not wanting the feeling of having not bought it. Sale countdowns, limited editions, and "only 3 left" notices are all designed to activate this driver directly.
☁️
Numbing
The quiet act of softening a difficult feeling through consumption. When the world feels too loud, too sharp, or too much, numbing is the act of turning the volume down. It's the internal response to overwhelming noise, and it shows up differently for everyone.
🌤️
Hope
Buying as a vote for a different future. When your present feels stuck, empty, or heavy, buying something new can feel like an investment in a better tomorrow. It’s the purchase version of thinking, “This might help… I might change… things might get easier.”

Why the combination is the thing that matters

Understanding triggers and core drivers separately is useful. Understanding how they combine — for you, specifically, in specific moments — is where the real shift happens.

The same trigger can activate completely different core drivers in different people, or in the same person on different days. A late-night sale email might activate FOMO for one person and reward-seeking in another. For someone else, that same email might land as an opportunity for escape on a hard evening, or comfort after a difficult week. The trigger is identical. What it touches is different every time.

This is why generic advice so often fails to hold. "Unsubscribe from sale emails" is sound in theory — but if the sale email wasn't really about the sale, if what it was activating was a need for comfort or escape or reward, the need will find another entry point. The sale email was just the messenger.

This is also why the if-then plan — a single, specific sentence that pre-decides a response to a specific combination — works when blanket rules don't. A rule that says "don't buy from sale emails" doesn't address the need. An if-then plan that says "when a late-night sale email arrives and I feel the urge to open it, I'll close my phone and make a drink instead" addresses the need directly, because it substitutes the behaviour that was meeting the need (browsing for escape or comfort) with something else that can meet it too.

from trigger + driver to if-then plan
If [trigger] meets [core driver], then I will [alternative that meets the same need].
Trigger: late-night browsing  +  Driver: escape  →  Plan: "When I'm scrolling at 10pm and feel the urge to add things to a basket, I'll close the app and watch something I already enjoy instead."

"Removing a trigger treats the symptom. Understanding which need it was activating, and finding a different way to meet that need — that's what changes the pattern."

A short exercise to find your combination

You don't need 30 days of data to start. A single honest reflection on a recent purchase can tell you a great deal. Work through this slowly, without judgement. The goal is curiosity, not verdict.

try this now
Decode a recent purchase
1
Choose something recent
A purchase from the last two weeks that felt at least partly automatic — something you bought without fully planning to, or something you almost bought and didn't. It doesn't need to be large.
Think of it clearly. Where were you? What time was it? What were you doing immediately before?
2
Identify the trigger
What set the urge off? Look at the ten triggers: browsing/scrolling, stress, boredom, late night, payday, advertising, social, email promotion, sale, habit. Most impulses have more than one trigger active at once — pick the one that feels most significant.
What was happening in the hour before the urge arose? Was there an environmental prompt — a notification, a scroll, a place — or was it an internal state like fatigue or stress?
3
Name the core driver
This is the harder question. Look at the ten core drivers: relief, reward, escape, comfort, validation, excitement, connection, control, identity, FOMO. Not what you were buying — what were you reaching for? What would getting the thing have given you, emotionally, in that moment?
If the purchase had arrived instantly and perfectly, how would you have felt? Relief? Excitement? Warmth? Safer? More yourself? That feeling is the driver.
4
Notice what this tells you
You now have a trigger and a driver. Even if you're not sure you've named them precisely, you have more information than you did before. The question to sit with is: was the purchase actually going to meet that need? And if not, what would?
Do this for a few more recent purchases and see whether the same combination keeps appearing. Most people find 2–3 dominant pairings that account for most of their impulse spending. That's your pattern.
what to do with this Once you have a clear trigger + driver combination, you can build a specific if-then plan for it — one sentence that pre-decides your response when that combination fires. The plan doesn't need to eliminate the urge. It just needs to redirect it toward something that meets the underlying need without the automatic purchase.
